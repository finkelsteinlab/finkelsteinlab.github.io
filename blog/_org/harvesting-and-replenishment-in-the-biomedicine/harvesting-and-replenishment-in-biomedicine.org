Since I work on CRISPR and retrons--bacterial defense systems with a reverse transcriptase (RTs)--several people sent me Anthropic's big reveal announcement. Their claim is that Claude "discovered" new RT enzymes with properties that are reminiscent of CRISPR.[fn:pr] The press release presents this as a step toward a new way of doing biology, with AI agents collaborating with humans at every stage. Not so fast!

I welcome the faster searches. My concern is how we replenish what they
search, and who learns to do the next round of science. If depositing data
means being scooped by the afternoon, people will hold it back. If we
automate a student's apprenticeship, we need another way to teach
scientific judgment. We can get better at harvesting discoveries while
making it harder to produce the next crop.

The result itself deserves attention. I would be pleased if a student
brought me this paper's finding. I would also have a substantial list of
experiments for them.

* An interesting system, with its function still to come

A reverse transcriptase (RT) copies RNA into DNA. HIV uses one, and so do
many of the kits in my building. Bacteria have their own RTs, including
those in defensive systems called retrons. These combine an RT, a short
RNA that it copies into DNA, and a partner protein. In the defensive
arrangement described here, the RT and its DNA product keep that partner
inactive. A phage infection disturbs the complex and releases the partner
to kill the cell before the virus can finish multiplying. Bad for that
bacterium; useful for its neighbors.

The new systems have the same general parts, but with an unusual RNA
component. Instead of one dedicated RNA, there is an array of three to
twenty-one repeats separated by unique spacers, each 120 to 220 nucleotides
long. The authors call these array-associated reverse transcriptases, or
ARTs.[fn:pr2]

That arrangement resembles CRISPR, but the spacers are four to seven times
longer, the repeat conservation differs, and there are no nearby /cas/
genes. The resemblance is a reason to investigate, and the authors are
appropriately careful about what it means.

There is experimental evidence beyond the sequence pattern. In published
sequencing data from a /Staphylococcus/ phage infection, the arrays account
for up to eight percent of phage RNA at fifteen minutes. Put the system on
a plasmid in /E. coli/ and you recover distinct short RNAs, with reproducible
boundaries. There is an RT, an associated partner gene, and an array that
produces RNAs. Something is happening here.

The proposed explanation is that one RT and one effector can work with a
repertoire of RNAs, perhaps responding to different triggers. That would
be an interesting addition to the immune-system collection. But nobody has
yet shown that this system aborts an infection. Anthropic acknowledges that
its function remains unknown. I would happily put a trainee on it for the
next two years, which should tell you both how promising I find it and how
much work remains.

* A day's work

The search took 21.5 hours of wall time: 119 tasks, 949 agent sessions, 77
agent-hours, and 216 million tokens. The agents searched 1.9 billion
protein clusters, recovered about 200,000 RTs, sorted them into nine
classes, and scored 3,564 neighboring protein families.[fn:campaign]

More interestingly, a supervisor agent opened 98 of those 119 tasks in
response to findings along the way. The search kept generating its own
follow-ups.

ART came from a hypothesis that failed. A worker rejected an apparent
association between an RT and a phage RNA polymerase subunit, but thought
a free-standing retron-like RT in a jumbo phage was worth another look.
The next worker inspected the upstream DNA:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
The L0050 (228,907 bp logan contig) flank is spectacular: I can see by eye
a *tandem repeat array*: "CATGTGTATCGCATGTT" / "CATGTGTTTCGCATGT"
repeating many times with ~100-180 bp spacers --- that's a CRISPR-like or
msDNA-like repeat array?!

--- Worker agent, task t0062[fn:t0062]
#+END_QUOTE

It then searched for prior descriptions, counted the repeats with a
script, and checked that the spacers were unrelated. Those are sensible
things to do after getting excited about a sequence. Quite a few exciting
sequences do not survive them.

Of seventeen candidate partner families investigated in depth, three
survived. The others were annotation artifacts, known systems, or chance
neighbors. A perfectly respectable yield, and a useful number to keep
beside the headline.

I have seen people say that any graduate student could have done this.
Having run similar projects, I think a junior student could spend a year
or more on it and still miss the signal. I am not sure a standard repeat
finder would catch these long spacers and imperfect repeats, either.
Building profiles, curating hits, and inspecting gene neighborhoods consume
months. Automating that work is valuable. I intend to use it.

The brief still matters. Experts chose RTs, wrote the selection criteria,
and used a tournament to rank nineteen reports before choosing a wet-lab
follow-up. These were unusually well-qualified experts, too.[fn:bios]
Peter Yoon trained in Doudna's lab and worked on mining ancestral Cas13
sequences. Januka Athukoralage worked on CRISPR signaling and viral
countermeasures. Nicholas Perry and Matthew Durrant worked on programmable
recombinases; Durrant also led computational biology on Evo. Several had
already worked together on the bridge recombinase project.

The other authors bring AI and company-building experience: Eric
Kauderer-Abrams leads biology and life sciences, and Emmanuel Ameisen works
on interpretability. The paper's claim that particular internal model
signals explain the DNA-repeat recognition deserves its own scrutiny.
But the biological expertise behind the search is easy to explain. Four
of six authors are molecular biologists with precisely the relevant
training. Give that group a great deal of compute and I would expect
something worth reading.

* The provenance question

The /New York Times/ reports that a Copenhagen group had been studying
something matching this finding and discussing it with Claude.[fn:nyt]
I do not know whether those conversations influenced the discovery. That
is a serious allegation, and convergence is quite plausible: many labs
are searching the same public databases for anti-phage systems.

It should be possible to investigate. The search code exists, and any
steering toward this clade might have left traces. It would also be
interesting to ask whether the unsuccessful candidates overlap unusually
often with work discussed by Claude users. That would take more evidence
than we have here.

Until then, I am agnostic. I will also keep treating unpublished ideas
entered into a commercial model as information that might inform the
company's products. That is the precaution I would recommend to a trainee,
without claiming to know what happened in this case.

* What grows back

Terence Tao has been considering a related problem in mathematics. He
welcomes automated searches that resolve tractable problems in the Erdős
database: they get useful work done and help identify the harder
questions.[fn:tao1][fn:tao2] I agree. Nobody needs to preserve a backlog for
its own sake.

His concern is what a solution leaves behind. In a discussion of an AI
solving Navier-Stokes regularity inside a company, he argues that the
failed approaches, the obstructions, and the adjustments could teach us
more than the final proof. A solution whose process cannot be examined
might add little to mathematics, even while disposing of a famous
problem.[fn:tao3][fn:tao4]

Biology differs in an obvious way. We want the answers themselves. A cure
that teaches us very little is still a cure, and I doubt the patients will
object. But we also need to keep producing questions, data, and people who
can make sense of either.

Consider the student whose year of sequence analysis I would like to
shorten. Some of that year is wasted on tedious work. Some is spent
learning to recognize a strange locus and deciding whether it deserves
another experiment. Automate the work and we need another way to teach
that judgment. I do not yet know what it is.

Then there are the databases. Colleagues who do environmental sequencing
and bioprospecting have become noticeably less willing to release data
before they have finished with it. My own university has taken an interest
in our datasets that it did not previously have. These are conversations
I have had, not a survey, but the incentive is plain enough. If someone
can mine your deposit in an afternoon, you have a reason to delay the
deposit. Fast searches will be less useful if the data stop arriving.

And there is the choice of questions. The ART agents followed an unexpected
lead, which counts in their favor. Their follow-ups were still inside an
expert's brief, and humans chose which report went to the bench. We have
good evidence here for pursuing surprises within a promising area. How
well these campaigns will open up areas that nobody thought to put in the
brief remains to be seen.

That is what I mean by harvesting and replenishment. I want the useful
findings extracted from public data, ART included. I also want the next
round of data deposited, the next students trained, and the unpromising
questions given enough attention to surprise us. A faster search does not
settle any of those problems.

* Back to the bench

Feng Zhang calls this an example of how AI agents can "contribute to
biological discovery."[fn:zhang] That seems right. There is a good result
here, and I would rather use these tools than assign my students a year of
work that can be compressed into a day.

I would also like the AI labs to publish the search harness, the failed
branches, and the fourteen candidates that washed out. Other people need
to be able to learn from the search, including the parts that did not make
the announcement.

Meanwhile, ART needs its function established. The agents have supplied
plenty of experiments to do. Cells still grow at the speed they grow.
