# Literature notes for "My token limit" (2026-09-04)

Two agent reports, condensed. A third (post-editing throughput, keystroke logging, reviewer time surveys) was cut off by the Opus session limit; gaps noted at the end.

## A. Daily capacity for cognitive work

### Deliberate practice / four productive hours
- Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993, Psych Rev 100:363. doi:10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363. Violinists ~3.5 h/day solo practice in 60-90 min blocks; "no benefit beyond 4 h/day, reduced benefit beyond 2 h"; cites novelists writing 4 h mornings (via Cowley 1959 and Plimpton 1977, the Paris Review interviews). n=10/group, one week of diaries.
- Macnamara & Maitra 2019, R Soc Open Sci 6:190327. Direct replication, n=39: best violinists practiced non-significantly less than good (d=-0.38).
- Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014, Psych Sci 25:1608. Deliberate practice explains 21% variance music, 26% games, 18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions.
- Pencavel 2015, Econ J 125:2052. doi:10.1111/ecoj.12166. Output sub-proportional above ~49 h/wk; 70+ h produced less total than 50-60 h.
- Van Zelst & Kerr 1951, J Abn Soc Psych 46:470. Source of Pang's inverted-U. Cross-sectional self-report.
- Kellogg 1986, Res High Educ 25:147. 121 sci/eng faculty: common habits uncorrelated with productivity; quiet environment the only typical correlate. "Before lunch, 1-2 h sessions" claim comes via Ericsson 1993 p. 392, not the abstract.
- Hartley & Branthwaite 1989, Higher Educ 18:423. 88 productive British psychologists; thinkers vs doers, anxious vs enthusiastic.

### Boice and the daily-writing literature
- Boice 1983, Behav Res Ther 21:537. doi:10.1016/0005-7967(83)90045-1. n=27 completers (9/group), 10 weeks. Krashen 2002 (Education 122:605, sdkrashen.com/content/articles/optimal_levels.pdf) re-analysis: spontaneous baseline 0.3-0.4 pages/day; daily-no-contingency 0.9; contingency ($15 to despised org) 3.2. Ideas per page: spontaneous 0.8, regular 0.7, forced 0.43. 15 of 42 dropped out (36%), all but one citing "the nuisance of charting." Ideas capped at four per day by design.
- Boice 1990, Professors as Writers, New Forums Press, pp. 79, 82-84: actual source of the famous 0.2/0.9/3.2 numbers (Silvia's figure cites this book).
- Boice 1989, BRAT 27:605: 10 procrastinators mentored twice weekly by Boice; source of 17/64/157 pages/year.
- Boice 1997, Written Commun 14:435. 16 volunteers, 8 bingers vs 8 regulars, observational. Weekly office visits recording pacing, pauses >=1 min, errors; blinded second coder (96% agreement). Ideas/week: regulars 7.3 vs bingers 1.6, t(14)=-9.0. Bingers 2-12 h sessions; regulars <=1 h, rarely >90 min. Pages/month only graphed.
- Boice 1991, New Dir Teach Learn 48:111. Quick starters (5-9% of new faculty): >=3 h/week writing by second semester; 1.5 manuscripts/year. Prescription: 30-60 min/workday.
- Sword 2016, Int J Acad Dev 21:312. doi:10.1080/1360144X.2016.1210153. 100 interviews + 1,223 questionnaires, 15 countries: 12.8% of exemplary writers and 11.5% of others write every weekday. Only 2 of 100 resembled Boice's regime. Charges: never replicated, undisclosed attrition, numbers "appear nowhere in the original article," no longitudinal follow-up, deficit model (only ~12% blocked per Boice & Johnson 1984).
- Sword 2017, Air & Light & Time & Space, Harvard UP. BASE framework.
- Silvia 2007/2019, How to Write a Lot, APA. No original data. Tracks yes/no "did I write"; word goals 50-250/day. Three uncorrelated daily dimensions: vexation, quality, quantity.
- Gray 2005, Publish & Flourish, NMSU. Step 2: write daily 15-30 min; step 3: record minutes daily, share weekly. Logs minutes, not words. Gray, Madson & Jackson 2018, To Improve Acad 37:243: N=93, self-reported extrapolated submissions ~2 to ~6/year, no control.
- Sarnecka et al. 2022, Innov High Educ 47:155. doi:10.1007/s10755-021-09574-6. Preregistered RCT, N=32 doctoral students, waitlist crossover. Time writing (RescueTime): no interaction (F=0.87, p=0.36). Quality: slight drop. Believed minimum productive block fell 41.7 to 17.4 min (p<0.00001). Lost 39% of RescueTime observations to the app silently stopping after OS updates.
- Porritt, Burt & Poling 2006, JABA 39:393. N=10 fiction writers; daily emailed manuscripts, counted in Word. Only clean objective-output design.
- McGrail, Rickard & Jones 2006, HERD 25:19. Systematic review, 17 studies 1984-2004: ~1.1 publications/person/year post-intervention; support groups > courses > coaches.
- Wallace & Pear 1977, JABA 10:515. Novelists' self-recording charts as fixed-ratio schedules; Trollope, Hemingway, Irving Wallace's own chart.

### Mental fatigue and time on task
- Boksem, Meijman & Lorist 2006, Biol Psych 72:123. 2 h Simon task: errors 9.2 to 12.9%; immediately-corrected errors 73% to 39%. 2005, Cogn Brain Res 25:107: 3 h, misses 11.5 to 19.7%. A EUR25 incentive block restored hour-one performance.
- Hockey 1997, Biol Psych 45:73; Hockey 2013, The Psychology of Fatigue, CUP. Compensatory control: primary output protected, costs appear as abandoned checking, simplified strategy, narrowed attention.
- Boksem & Tops 2008, Brain Res Rev 59:125. Kurzban et al. 2013, BBS 36:661 (opportunity cost). Inzlicht, Shenhav & Olivola 2018, TiCS 22:337.
- Ego depletion: Hagger et al. 2016, PPS 11:546 (23 labs, N=2141, d=0.04); Vohs et al. 2021, Psych Sci 32:1566 (36 labs, N=3531, d=0.06).
- Vigilance: Mackworth 1948, QJEP 1:6; See et al. 1995, Psych Bull 117:230. Low-information monitoring; don't extrapolate to writing.
- Wiehler et al. 2022, Curr Biol 32:3564. 6.25 h task, lPFC glutamate accumulation, shift to low-effort choices. Single study; subjective fatigue didn't differ.
- Landrigan et al. 2004, NEJM 351:1838: 36% more serious errors on 24 h+ shifts. iCOMPARE 2019, NEJM: no mortality difference.
- Albulescu et al. 2022, PLoS ONE 17:e0272460. Micro-breaks: vigor d=0.36, performance d=0.16 ns.
- Sievertsen, Gino & Piovesan 2016, PNAS 113:2621. Danish test scores -0.9% SD per hour later; 20-30 min break +1.7% SD.

### Decision fatigue in evaluators
- Danziger et al. 2011, PNAS 108:6889. Refuted: Weinshall-Margel & Shapard 2011, PNAS 108:E833 (non-random order); Glöckner 2016, JDM 11:601 (implied d~1.96; rational-stopping artifact).
- Linder et al. 2014, JAMA Intern Med 174:2029. 21,867 visits; inappropriate antibiotics hour 4 vs 1 aOR 1.26. Hsiang et al. 2019, JAMA Netw Open 2:e193403 (screening orders 63.7% to 47.8%). Kim et al. 2018, JAMA Netw Open 1:e181770 (flu vax 44 to 32%). Neprash & Barnett 2019, JAMA Netw Open 2:e1910373 (opioids rise, NSAIDs flat: placebo test). Dissent: Andersson et al. 2025, Commun Psychol 3:33 (null).
- Peer review: Pier et al. 2018, PNAS 115:2952 (ICC=0) with Erosheva et al. 2021, JRSS-A 184:904 (range restriction; full-range IRR 0.34). Gallo et al. 2016, PLoS ONE 11:e0165147 (77% variance reviewer/residual). Callaham & McCulloch 2011, Ann Emerg Med 57:141 (14,808 reviews; 92% of reviewers deteriorate, -0.8%/yr). Black et al. 1998, JAMA 280:231 (review quality plateaus after ~3 h). Kovanis et al. 2016, PLoS ONE 11:e0166387 (4-5 h per manuscript; 20% of researchers do 69-94% of reviews).
- No NIH study-section order study exists. Criscuolo et al. 2021, Org Sci 32:987: randomized R&D proposal order; proposal after a funded one gets 23% less, 55% less late in meeting.
- Simonsohn & Gino 2013, Psych Sci 24:219 (MBA interviews, ~0.08 SD contrast). Chen, Moskowitz & Shue 2016, QJE 131:1181. Zhao et al. 2017, Front Psych 8:933 (67,500 essays: assimilation). Ling, Mollaun & Xi 2014, Lang Test 31:479.

### Reading, proofreading, editing throughput
- Brysbaert 2019, J Mem Lang 109:104047. Non-fiction silent 238 wpm (SD 51); fiction 260; aloud 183; reading-for-recall ~100. Dirix et al. 2019: 54 wpm studying vs 189 reading.
- Rayner et al. 2016, PSPI 17:4. Speed reading = skimming; comprehension 81 to 74%.
- Strukelj & Niehorster 2018, J Eye Mov Res 11(1). Proofreading slower, worse comprehension.
- Daneman & Stainton 1993, Read Writ 5:297. Own text 81% nonword / 59% word errors caught; others' 89/76%. Riefer 1991: difficult text 47-58%.
- EFA 2026 rate chart: proofreading 1,500-5,000 w/h; copyediting 1,125-3,000; developmental 750-3,375; default ~1,250 w/h.

### Writing output and process
- Flower & Hayes 1981, CCC 32:365; Hayes 2012, Writ Commun 29:369. Process models, no rates.
- Kellogg 2008, J Writ Res 1:1; Kellogg 2001, AJP 114:175 (translating 40-50% of time). Levy & Ransdell 1995, Mem Cogn 23:767.
- Kaufer, Hayes & Flower 1986, RTE 20:121: ~9-word bursts. Karat et al. 1999, CHI: transcription 32.5 wpm, composition 19 wpm (~1,100 w/h continuous).
- Chenoweth & Hayes 2001, Writ Commun 18:80. Burst = words between >=2 s pauses; P-bursts vs R-bursts; with experience, words/min and burst length rise and more proposed words survive.
- Leijten & Van Waes 2013, Writ Commun 30:358. Inputlog keystroke logging: pause, revision, source-integration analysis.
- Wieth & Zacks 2011, Think Reason 17:387. N=428: analytic problems no time-of-day effect; insight better at NON-optimal time. May, Hasher & Healey 2023, PPS (synchrony effect in 45% of young-adult studies vs 83% older). Rey-Mermet & Rothen 2023, Collabra 9:88337 (null).

### Teaching
- Bradbury 2016, Adv Physiol Educ 40:509; Wilson & Korn 2007, Teach Psych 34:85. 10-15 min attention span is a myth.
- Bunce, Flens & Neiles 2010, J Chem Educ 87:1438. Clicker lapses at 30 s, 4.5 min, 7-9 min; no cliff; instructor variance > timeline.
- Instructor cognitive load per contact hour: nothing quantitative. Only vocal load (~16% phonation time; 11% voice disorders vs 6.2%).

### Knowledge-worker attention
- González & Mark 2004, CHI (~3 min per event, ~12 min per sphere). Mark, González & Harris 2005, CHI (25 min 26 s = elapsed return time, not recovery). Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008, CHI. Mark et al. 2016, CHI (median 40 s screen focus).
- Leroy 2009, OBHDP 109:168 (attention residue; no registered replication).
- DeskTime 52/17 (2014), later 80/17 (2020) and 112/26 (2021); no n ever published. RescueTime 2019: 2 h 48 min productive/day, 185 M hours, self-selected.
- Robinson et al. 2011, Monthly Labor Rev June:43. People overestimate work hours 5-10%, more the longer the estimate (55-59 h claimed: ~10 h over).
- Korotitsch & Nelson-Gray 1999, Psych Assess 11:415. Self-monitoring is reactive; first month of logging is not a baseline.
- Reynolds, Robles & Repetti 2016, Dev Psych 52:442. 56-day daily diary, >90% compliance achievable.

### Throughput as a budget; the token analogy
- Kahneman 1973, Attention and Effort. Simon 1971, "poverty of attention." Shenhav, Botvinick & Cohen 2013, Neuron (EVC).
- Zheng & Meister 2025, Neuron, arXiv:2408.10234. Human behavioral throughput ~10 bits/s; reading 28-45; typing 10; sensory input ~1e9 bits/s.
- Frank 2023, TiCS 27:990. Children: 9-110 M words input; 200-400 M by 20. BabyLM (Warstadt et al. 2023): 100 M word budget.
- Arithmetic: 1 M tokens ~750 k words = ~52 h continuous reading at 238 wpm, ~125 h at 100 wpm.

## B. Self-quantification prior art

### Writers' logs (verified against primary texts where marked)
- Trollope, An Autobiography (1883), Gutenberg #5978. Ch. VII: diary of pages/week, average 40, range 20-112, page = 250 words, "every word counted." Ch. XV: 250 words per quarter hour with watch, 3 h/day, first 30 min re-reading yesterday's work; at desk 5:30 am, groom paid GBP5/yr to wake him. Ch. XIX: 66 pages/week for 8 weeks at sea. Peak rate 1,000 w/h; committed quota 40-60% of that.
- King, On Writing (2000): 10 pages = 2,000 words/day for himself; advises 1,000/day, six days a week. "Every day except Christmas" was "a lie."
- Greene: Paris Review No. 3 (1953): 500, stepped up to 750 as the book progresses. 1985 interview (JSSE 41): dropped to 300-350. The famous "averaged five hundred words a day for five days a week, mark off the hundreds" is Bendrix in The End of the Affair (1951), fiction.
- Hemingway: Paris Review No. 18 (1958). Plimpton's narration: chart on cardboard, "so as not to kid myself," 450, 575, 462, 1250, 512. Mean 650, median 512. High days bought fishing days.
- Twain, North American Review 1907, Gutenberg #19987: 3,000 words/day in 1868 (7-9 h), 1,800 in 1897, 1,400 per 4-5 h sitting in 1904; catches his own failure to normalize by hours; "figures often beguile me."
- Angelou: Paris Review No. 116 (1990). Hotel room 6:30 to 12:30-1:30; 9 pages gross, saves 2.5-3 (~30% keep rate). Only writer reporting yield.
- NaNoWriMo: 1,667/day; win rate 11-14% in the 2010s; official "29%" used only those who wrote anything (57% of registrants wrote zero). Non-finishers average <=~4,500 words (~2.7 days of pace): attrition front-loaded. RescueTime 2018 cohort: winners consistent in first four days; 30% of winners vs 73% of non-winners missed two consecutive days. Org dissolved 31 Mar 2025; no dataset preserved.

### Quantified-self
- Jamie Todd Rubin: 825-day streak (2013-2015), 687,907 words, ~833/day, ~42 min/day, time-to-words r=0.59; Google Apps Script nightly diff + RescueTime API. Modal day 500-600 words. Strain accumulated linearly, noticeable ~day 600; ended voluntarily. github.com/jamietr1/google-docs-writing-tracker.
- 750words.com (Benson): 861,801 registered; 68 users at 2,000+ day streaks. Auto-metrics: elapsed time, typing time, wpm, pauses >=3 min as "distractions."
- Gwern: randomized self-blinded, mostly null (caffeine n=441 days; magnesium n=767, negative; LSD microdose d=-0.18 ns). Power by simulation. Never used word count. 2026 interview: "mostly lost faith in the measurements"; productive days anti-correlated with his metrics; interactions need ~16x data.
- Wolfram 2012, 2019: >100 M keystrokes, backspaces ~7% stable; 25,000 characters/day into notebooks; daily automated digest.
- Felton annual reports 2005-2014: 2013 logged 94,842 conversations / 7.67 M words; skipped conversations to avoid logging them; "when you write the memory enough, it becomes the memory."
- Buster Benson: objective and subjective in separate columns; one boolean daily criterion ("did meaningful work").
- Seth Roberts 2004, BBS 27:227. doi:10.1017/S0140525X04000068. 12 years of self-experiment; value is hypothesis generation from long series; ABA reversals; n=880 days.
- Nick Winter, The Motivation Hacker (2013): experience sampling every ~3 h; Beeminder pledges to $7,290; readers: "almost no one did anything that lasted more than a week."
- Ben Kuhn, benkuhn.net/50pct: 93 to 131 productive engineering h/month at constant screen time; undetectable by memory. Write the questions before choosing instruments.
- Cal Newport: index-card deep-work tally; abandoned for deep scheduling.
- Beeminder URLminder: odometer (monotone total) word counts, robust to cutting.

### Reporting standards
- CENT 2015 (Shamseer et al., J Clin Epi / BMJ): CONSORT extension for N-of-1.
- SCRIBE 2016 (Tate et al., PMC4960517): 26-item single-case checklist.

## C1. Post-editing throughput (Sonnet agent, 2026-09-04)

- Krings 2001, Repairing Texts, Kent State UP. Three effort dimensions: temporal, technical (keystrokes, insert/delete/shift), cognitive. Time is a proxy for cognitive effort. Think-aloud, small N, no words/hour.
- Snover et al. 2006, AMTA, aclanthology.org/2006.amta-papers.25. TER = (insert+delete+substitute+shift) / reference words. HTER: human makes a minimally edited reference from the MT output first. Standard technical-effort metric.
- O'Brien 2006, Across Lang Cult 7:1 (pauses as cognitive effort). O'Brien 2011 (eye tracking).
- Koponen et al. 2012, WMT7 (W12-3123); Koponen 2016, Mach Transl 30 (doi:10.1007/s10590-015-9175-2): perceived effort correlates weakly with measured edits; long segments feel harder than they are.
- Läubli et al. 2013, WPTP2: post-editing beats fully equipped TM workbench on time and quality. Läubli et al. 2014 AMTA-WPTP "Perception vs reality".
- Tatsumi 2009/2010, MT Summit XII: predicting PE time from MT metrics + source complexity.
- Guerberof 2009; thesis 2014; Mach Transl 2014: PE of SMT ~ processing 85-94% fuzzy matches; both beat from-scratch; experience affects speed not quality.
- Green, Heer & Manning 2013, CHI, doi:10.1145/2470654.2470718. Within-subject, En->Ar/Fr/De: post-editing faster AND higher quality than from scratch. Exact seconds/sentence not retrieved.
- Plitt & Masselot 2010, Prague Bull Math Ling 93:7. 12 translators, 2 days: +74% productivity vs from scratch, fewer errors in post-edited output. Most-cited number.
- Industry anchors (not peer-reviewed): from-scratch translation ~250-600 w/h, ~300 typical, 2,000-3,000 words/day. TAUS DQF 2019 industry mean post-editing ~1,057 w/h; light PE ~1,000 w/h, full PE ~600-800 w/h. Literary NMT PE ~685 w/h (+36%). ISO 18587:2017 specifies process, not throughput.
- Fatigue: no controlled study of PE throughput vs session length or time of day. Proxies: interpreters rotate every 20-30 min, long pauses increase over session; office typing study (PLoS ONE 2020, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0239984): morning sessions keep speed at cost of more corrections, later sessions lose both.
- LLM era: Noy & Zhang 2023, Science 381, doi:10.1126/science.adh2586 (ChatGPT RCT, professional writing: time down, quality up, gains concentrated in weaker writers). arXiv 2511.12529 (IUI 2026) "Accepted with minor revisions": authors make smaller Levenshtein edits to AI-generated abstracts than to human ones; perceived authorship drives edit size. arXiv 2604.24444 (2026): 81 participants post-editing LLM drafts pull style partway back. Humans alter ~52% of segments; LLM post-editors rewrite 87-99%. medRxiv 2025.08.23.25334280: erroneous LLM output degrades physician accuracy even when checking.
- Proofreading anchor: Spirrison & Gordy 1993: undergraduates missed ~24% of 49 seeded errors in 385 words.
- Takeaways: log time, edit distance, and rated difficulty separately; use word-level TER between draft and final; anchor ~300 w/h from scratch vs 700-1,200 w/h editing a decent draft; seed errors to catch automation bias (throughput looks best exactly when you miss most); track position in session.

## C2. Keystroke logging and within-session dynamics (Sonnet agent, 2026-09-04)

- Tools: Inputlog (Leijten & Van Waes 2013, Writ Commun 30:358, doi:10.1177/0741088313491692); ScriptLog (Strömqvist, Wengelin); Translog-II (Carl 2012, LREC, aclanthology L12-1352); CyWrite (github.com/chukharev/cywrite). Standard pause threshold 2 s; P-bursts vs R-bursts; product/process ratio = final words / words produced.
- Kaufer, Hayes & Flower 1986, RTE 20:121: ~9-word bursts. Chenoweth & Hayes 2001, Writ Commun 18:80. Hayes & Chenoweth 2006/2007, Writ Commun 23:135 and 24(4): bursts rare in pure transcription. Burst length and pause duration predict fluency and quality; sensitive to threshold.
- Pauses: Wengelin 2006 (Studies in Writing 18, Elsevier); Schilperoord 1996; Spelman Miller 2006: pause length scales with unit planned (sentence > clause > constituent > word). Van Waes & Leijten 2015, Comput Compos 38:79.
- Time course: Ransdell & Levy 1996 (load drops fluency 17.0 to 14.6 wpm; 6-digit load drops quality 75% to 63%). Kellogg 1996 (working memory model). Olive, Kellogg & Piolat triple-task: planning effort high early and declining, revision effort rising across a session. Torrance et al. 1999. No keystroke study plots burst length or wpm decay over a continuous 60-120 min session. Gap.
- Typing: Dhakal et al. 2018, CHI, doi:10.1145/3173574.3174220, 168,000 participants, 136 M keystrokes: error rate vs speed r=-0.36; baseline correction rates. Single session. No peer-reviewed hours-of-typing decline data found.
- LLM co-writing: Lee, Liang & Yang 2022, CHI, CoAuthor, doi:10.1145/3491102.3502030 (63 writers, 1,445 sessions, full logs). arXiv 2604.11009 (ownership by stage); arXiv 2403.01055; DraftMarks CHI 2026 doi:10.1145/3772318.3791109 (provenance traces); arXiv 2604.12097 "temporal flattening" (preprint, unverified); ScholaWrite 2025 (unverified).
- Takeaways: log raw inter-keystroke intervals so bursts can be rederived; bin burst length and revision count by 10-min windows; log pause location; backspace rate per 100 chars as effort proxy; expect reallocation (planning to revision) rather than monotonic decline.

## C3. Reviewer time and load (Sonnet agent, 2026-09-04)

- Ware & Monkman 2008, PRC "Peer review in scholarly journals". Active reviewers 14.3 papers/yr at mean 6.8 h; typical researcher ~8 papers/yr, mean 8.5 h, median 5 h per review. Origin of the "5 hours" figure.
- Sense About Science 2019 survey: mode 4 h, median 6 h per review (secondary summaries).
- Mulligan, Hall & Raphael 2013, JASIST 64:132, doi:10.1002/asi.22798: >4,000 respondents; hours/review not confirmed.
- Publons/Clarivate 2018 Global State of Peer Review: median turnaround 16.4 days; ~68.5 M reviewer-hours/yr; "10% of reviewers do 50% of reviews" (secondary). No hours-per-review figure verified.
- Kovanis et al. 2016, PLoS ONE 11:e0166387: modeling on MEDLINE; 20% of researchers do 69-94% of reviews; 70% of active reviewers spend <=1% of work time reviewing, 5% spend >=13%.
- Aczel, Szaszi & Holcombe 2021, Res Integr Peer Rev 6:14, doi:10.1186/s41073-021-00118-2: >100 M reviewer-hours in 2020; >$1.5 B US.
- Grant review: no NIH CSR hours-per-application figure found. Practitioner estimates 2-4 h per application, 6-15 applications per reviewer per round, 5-15 min panel discussion each. Martin, Kopstein & Janice 2010, PLoS ONE 5:e13526: preliminary vs post-discussion score r=0.78; 13% of top-scoring apps moved enough to affect funding.
- Herbert et al. 2013, BMJ Open 3:e002800: 285 researchers, 632 NHMRC proposals: mean 34 working days per proposal (38 new, 28 resubmission); 21% success. Herbert, Barnett & Graves 2013, Nature 495:314: ~550 person-years/yr.
- von Hippel & von Hippel 2015, PLoS ONE 10:e0118494: 116 PI-hours + 55 co-I hours per proposal; time uncorrelated with success.
- Guthrie, Ghiga & Wooding 2018, F1000Research 6:1335 (RAND): evidence base on grant review thin.
- Black et al. 1998, JAMA 280:231: 420 BMJ manuscripts; quality rises with time to ~3 h, not beyond (paraphrase widely repeated; coefficient not re-verified).
- Callaham & Tercier 2007, PLoS Med 4:e40: 306 reviewers, 2,856 reviews; only <10 yr post-training and teaching-hospital affiliation predicted quality.
- Fang, Bowen & Casadevall 2016, eLife 5:e13323 (not PNAS): 102,740 grants; percentile poorly predicts productivity in fundable range.
- Document lengths: median research article 4,133 words (n=61,519; 90% within 2,023-8,284). R01 aims ~500-600 words + research strategy 12 pages ~6,000-7,000 words.
- Benchmarks: 4-6 h per manuscript review; diminishing returns after ~3 h; an R01 is ~6,500 words evaluated in 2-4 h; top 20% of reviewers carry most of the load; a proposal costs ~34 working days against ~20% success.

## C. Gaps
- No controlled study of post-editing or writing throughput vs session length. No NIH hours-per-application survey. No 2023-2026 hours-per-review update found.

## D. Phantom or misattributed claims to avoid
Hanna et al. pathologist fatigue; "Lee 2017 GIE meta-analysis"; Sattler IRR 0.61 to 0.89; "Kellogg: professionals write a few hundred words/hour"; "CIEP 3,000 words/hour"; Greene's 500-words quote (fiction); Trollope "started next novel same day" (text: "on the next"); Gray step 1 as "write 15 min first" (it is urgent vs important; daily writing is step 2, 15-30 min); Boice "spontaneous = 0.9" (that was the daily-no-contingency arm).

## F. My own Claude Code logs (mined 2026-09-04; sessions.py, plots_sessions.py, figS1-S4)

Claude Code transcripts (~/.claude/projects) retain ~30 days, so the window is Aug 5 to Sep 4 2026, not three months. Codex transcripts (~/.codex/sessions, main threads only; guardian and spawned subagent threads excluded) go back to June but are restricted to the same window. 258 sessions with at least one typed prompt (16 Codex), 3,108 prompts (129 Codex), 31 active days. Codex is 3% of visible tokens in the window; it was heavier in June-July.
- Sessions per active day: median 8, max 22. Prompts per day: 20-200.
- Visible tokens (assistant prose + tool call headers + 3-line result previews, 4 chars/token): median 6,600 per session, 108,000 per active day; model prose alone median 44,000/day. Split of visible: prose 38%, headers 12%, previews 50%.
- Hidden per session (thinking, tool inputs, rest of results, agent reports): median 28,600, i.e. 4-5x the visible.
