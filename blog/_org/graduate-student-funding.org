Happy start of the semester to all who celebrate!

This Fall, I am teaching MOL 391: Grant Writing and Scientific Presentations, a
seminar for second-year ILS students who spend the semester writing a two- or
three-aim proposal in the style of an NIH F31 fellowship on their own
dissertation project.

The first day of class, I asked the students how well they understand how their salary, tuition, and research expenses are funded. I certainly didn't understand any of this when I was a graduate student!  When I asked last year, some of the students were also unsure.

I think every student should understand how the money flows, where it comes
from, and who is actually paying for them. This is because funding is currently
being stripped from biomedicine and the US research enterprise in general.
International students are especially hard squeezed because they have much fewer
sources of funding, including independent funding. And science is much less fun
when you can't afford reagents and services, when you can't afford even the basic
reagents, and you have to spend your time doing things that can be outsourced to
companies.

So how do graduate students get funding? I thought I'd collect what I know here,
for anyone who is curious. The main reason for doing this is to educate students
on how the money flows, to give them some agency and understanding in talking to
their advisors about how their funding works. And most importantly, that students
have more control than they think: the best way to get scientific independence
is to get funding of your own.

So let's follow a sample graduate student, say, Jane Einstein. She comes into our
graduate program, the [[https://ils.utexas.edu/][Interdisciplinary Life Sciences
(ILS)]] program, in 2026, eager to start her research project.

When she gets here she'll have an opportunity to rotate in up to three different
research labs. During those rotations, the graduate program pays her salary,
fringe, and tuition. The program gets some of its funding from the university and
some from the individual departments which contribute to it.

Note that her time in the lab during these three rotations doesn't exactly align
with the semester system. So at some point these three rotations end and Jane
signs on to her research lab. She's excited to join Professor XYZ as they embark
on a study of how sourdough starters evolve when their owners go on vacation.

As soon as Jane signs on the dotted line, her expenses are transferred to her
advisor's funding. That funding can come from a number of sources: federal
grants, which is true for most of my colleagues and definitely me; private
foundation grants; the income from an endowment, if the professor is lucky enough
to have one, and I do not; and any kind of charitable giving or corporate money,
for example sponsored research projects.

So now Jane is fully on her advisor's accounts. The advisor pays all of the
research costs, which total to approximately $70,000 to $75,000 a year: $40,000
in salary, about $4,800 in fringe, $10,554 in tuition, and then the bench fees on
top. In my lab, the bench fees will of course depend on the type of project, but
as a rule of thumb I budget fifteen to twenty thousand dollars per person per
year, and that has to cover every research expense <note: none of my recent
proposals carries a per-person supplies line - ARPA-H, Cisterna and CFF all
budget supplies at the project level. The only per-person figure I found is the
RM1's $1,200 a month per experimentalist, which is $14,400 a year and roughly
matches this rule of thumb.>. That means every dollar Jane
costs now comes out of her advisor's funding. And this is how things will go for
Jane, assuming that her advisor's funding is stable and everything is copacetic.

But at some point her advisor will suggest that she work as a teaching assistant
(TA). Our graduate program sets a minimum, and for most graduate programs that
minimum is one long semester (fall or spring); some require two. And in some
graduate programs, notably chemistry, students are expected to TA much more.
Further, a TA appointment can be full time, which in our budgeting and accounting
system means only twenty hours a week, or half time, which means ten hours a week.
Twenty hours is also the threshold for
[[https://ils.utexas.edu/current-graduate-students/funding-employment][no-cost
health insurance]], so it is not a trivial line.

Either way, even a full-time TA appointment covers only the TA stipend, which
these days is much lower than the ILS stipend <note: I could not find a published
CNS or ILS TA rate for 2026. CNS says it sets standard TA and AI rates each year
but does not post the dollar figure; the only public number I found is Liberal
Arts, $21,175 for nine months in 2025-26, a different college. Fill in the real
number or drop the comparison.>. The TA stipend is lower because it's set by the
teaching office and not by our graduate office. So Jane's advisor
still needs to make up the difference between these two salaries. This is not
optional: [[https://cns.utexas.edu/graduate-education/college-policies/academic-employment][CNS
policy]] says a graduate salary can be no lower than the TA rate or the
first-year stipend, whichever is higher, and our program requires the PI to
supplement a TA's stipend so it stays in line with the first-year rate. So Jane
should not see a cut. If your program has no such rule, this is exactly the
paragraph to ask your advisor about.

But we're not done. What about the fringe and lab fees? Fringe will be covered by
the teaching office, so that no longer falls on Jane's advisor for that one long
semester. But the research expenses are still coming out of the advisor's grants.

So what happens if Jane's advisor has a lapse in funding? Well, the best case is
that Jane has already won her own support, covering some or all of her graduate
career. That means applying for independent graduate funding, and applying early.
It's much easier for US nationals, but international students also have some
private foundation opportunities. The best list I know of is the
[[https://research.jhu.edu/rdt/funding-opportunities/graduate/][Johns Hopkins
graduate funding database]], which is free, updated monthly, and not limited to
Hopkins students. More on that later.

But if Jane is ineligible or cannot secure independent funding, several less
palatable options remain. She can attempt to sign up for more TA appointments.
This will partially cover her salary and fringe, and hopefully her advisor still
has the funds to make up the gap in her salary and cover her bench fees.

She can attempt to join a different lab or be co-supervised by a different lab.
That would partially cover her expenses. Side note: this may be a good idea in
general for graduate students. Having two advisors can increase your headaches,
but also spreads the risk regarding funding.

And in the worst case scenario, Jane and her advisor both run out of money. Some
departments have short-term bridge funding, but that's not guaranteed: the
department chair decides.

Finally, if Jane and her advisor cannot find any additional support, then Jane
has to make the difficult decision of whether she wants to leave the graduate
program with a terminal master's degree. Even in that worst case, graduate
students have a cushion that postdocs and other staff scientists do not: the TA
safety net.

But nonetheless, here is the point: talk to your advisor about how you are paid,
and go after your own funding. Applying for grants builds a graduate student's
career in several ways, and I think students could use it far more often. More on
that later. But this just highlights one very strong reason for doing that, and
that is to get some financial protection and independence from the fluctuating
funding of your graduate advisor.
