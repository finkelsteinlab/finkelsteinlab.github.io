This fall I am teaching Molecular Biology 391, which is a graduate-student-level
grant writing course. The first day of class I always ask the students how well
they understand how their salary, tuition, and research expenses are actually
being funded. When I was a graduate student I certainly didn't understand all
three of these questions. And when I asked this question last year, many of the
students in my class had no clue either. This year I am happy to say that only a
few students didn't have a much clearer understanding of how their work is going
to be funded.

I personally think it is incredibly important in this day and age to understand
how the money flows, where the money comes from, and how each student's funding
is happening. This is because funding is currently being stripped from
biomedicine and the US research enterprise in general. International students are
especially hard squeezed because they have much fewer sources of funding,
including independent funding. And we all know that science is much less fun when
you can't afford reagents and services. You can't afford even the basic reagents.
You have to spend your time doing things that can be outsourced to companies.

So how do graduate students get funding? I thought I'd collect information here so
that any curious, I'll collect information. Please see below.

The main reason for doing this is to educate students on how the money flows, to
give them some agency and understanding in talking to their advisors about how
their funding works. And most importantly, to highlight that students have
control, and the best way to get scientific independence is to get funding
benefits.

So let's take a sample graduate student, say, Jane Einstein. She comes into our
graduate program in 2026, eager and excited to do her research project. When she
gets here she'll have an opportunity to rotate in up to three different research
labs. During that time her salary, fringe, and tuition are paid by the graduate
program, which gets some of its funding from the university and some of its
funding from the individual departments which contribute to that graduate
program.

Note that her time in the lab during these three rotations doesn't exactly align
with the semester system. So at some point these three rotations end and Jane
signs on to her research lab. She's very excited to join Professor XYZ as they
embark on a deep survey of the microbiome of their belly button. <LLM: update the

As soon as Jane signs on the dotted line, her expenses are transferred to her
advisor's funding. This funding can come from a number of sources, including
their federal grants, which is generally true for most of my colleagues and
definitely me; private foundation grants; endowments, the income generated by
that professor's endowment if they're lucky to have one, and I do not; and any
kind of charitable giving or corporate research, for example sponsored research
projects they may have. These are the general buckets that are available to us as
principal investigators to fund our research personnel.

But at some point her advisor will suggest that she GTA. Our graduate program has

But we're not done. What about the fringe and lab fees? Fringe will be covered by
the teaching office, so that no longer falls on the graduate advisor for that one
long semester. But the research expenses are still coming out of the graduate
advisor's grants.

So what happens if that graduate advisor has a lapse in funding? Well, the best
thing that can happen is that the graduate student is actually independently
supported for some or all of their graduate experience, and the best way to do
that is to apply for independent graduate support. More on that later.

But if that student is ineligible or was unable to secure independent funding,
then there's a slew of less palatable options available to them. They can attempt
to sign up for more TA opportunities. This will partially cover their salary and
fringe, and hopefully their graduate advisor still has the funds to make up the
gap in their salary and cover their bench fees.

They can attempt to join a different lab or be co-supervised by a different lab.
That would partially cover their expenses. Side note: this may be a good idea in
general for graduate students. Having two advisors can increase your headaches,
but also spreads the risk regarding funding.

And in the worst case scenario, the student and the advisor both run out of money.
Some departments have bridge funding for short periods of time, but that's not
guaranteed and very much dependent on what the department's chair decides in those
cases.

Finally, if the student and their advisor cannot find any additional support for
the student, then the student has to make the difficult decision of whether they
want to leave the graduate program with a master's terminal degree. This is of
course much better than the situation for postdocs and other staff scientists,
who don't have the TA safety net, so to speak.

But nonetheless, my central point here is to highlight how important it is for
students to engage their mentors regarding their funding situation, and ultimately
try to get independent funding. There are many reasons why applying for grants is
a wonderful career-building opportunity for graduate students, that I think can be
used more frequently. More on that later. But this just highlights one very strong
reason for doing that, and that is to get some financial protection and
independence from the fluctuating funding of your graduate advisor.
